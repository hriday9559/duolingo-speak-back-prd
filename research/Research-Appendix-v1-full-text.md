Rules for reporting results
    1.      Always report real counts as "X of N", for example "18 of 52 respondents." Add a % only next to the count, never
            on its own.
    2.      Be honest about the small sample. 50 survey responses and 5–6 interviews can show a direction. They cannot
            prove anything about Duolingo's 150M+ monthly users.
    3.      Say who you reached. My respondents will mostly be students and young professionals in India, from my own
            network. Write this down as a limitation.
    4.      Don't split small groups too far. If a segment has fewer than 10 people, describe it in words, not percentages.
    5.      Report what surprised you or went against your idea. This is more convincing than only supporting evidence.
         Note on sample size and bias: A small, self-selected sample (people who chose to answer, from my own
         network) is likely to over-represent people who are engaged, interested in languages, and similar to me. Treat
         every finding as a signal to test further, not as a fact. The real test of the idea is the A/B experiment in the PRD.

A3. How to analyze the responses
Survey
  1.     Clean the data. Remove blank or joke answers. Write down how many you removed and why.
  2.     Make three groups from Q3: Active, Lapsed (stopped), At-risk (thinking of stopping).
  3.     For each group, count:
            –    How many picked each reason in Q4.
            –    The spread of answers to Q5 and Q6 (how many chose 1, 2, 3, 4, 5).
            –    How many chose "Would like it" in Q8.
  4.     Test Problem 1: cross Q2 (time used) with Q5 (speaking rating). Do long-time users still rate speaking low?
  5.     Test format preference: count Q10 answers. Friends vs strangers? Voice messages vs live calls?
  6.     Write every result as "X of N." If a group is under 10 people, describe it in words only.

Interviews (affinity clustering)
  1.     After each interview, write each separate point on its own sticky note (Miro or FigJam). One idea per note.
  2.     Put all notes on one board. Group similar notes. Name each group in the user's words, e.g., "I freeze when people talk
         fast."
  3.     Count how many different people mention each group (not how many notes). With 5–6 interviews, write "4 of 6
         people."
  4.     Rank groups by number of people, then by how strongly they felt.
  5.     Tag each group: supports Problem 1, Problem 2, both, or neither.

Decide
  •      Give each hypothesis (H1–H4 in the PRD) a one-line verdict: Supported / Weak signal / Not supported.
  •      If Problem 2 is not supported, say so in the PRD's "What I learned / what changed" section and change direction.
         Changing your mind based on evidence is a strength.
